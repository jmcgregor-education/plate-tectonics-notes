Plate Tectonics: Theory, Evidence & Mechanism

In 1915, Alfred Wegener claimed the continents had moved, and he had good evidence. Most geologists still said no. It took a map of the seafloor, a magnetic barcode, and a new look at Earth’s interior heat to change their minds.

Part 1

Continents that fit together

Look at a world map and the east coast of South America seems to tuck into the west coast of Africa. People noticed this as soon as decent maps existed. In 1915, the German scientist Alfred Wegener went much further. He proposed continental drift: the idea that all the continents were once joined in a single supercontinent he called Pangaea (“all lands”), which later broke apart.

The fit alone wasn’t proof. Coastlines change as sea level rises and falls. (The fit is actually best along the edges of the continental shelves, underwater, which are the true edges of the continents.) So Wegener gathered other kinds of evidence.

Wegener’s evidence

Fossil evidence. Fossils of Mesosaurus, a small reptile that lived in fresh water, are found only in southern Africa and eastern South America. It couldn’t have swum across an ocean of salt water. Fossils of the land reptile Lystrosaurus and the seed fern Glossopteris turn up across Africa, India, Antarctica, and (for Glossopteris) South America and Australia, too.

Rock evidence. Rock belts of the same type and age line up across oceans. The Appalachian Mountains in North America match mountain belts in Scotland and Scandinavia. Very old rock regions in Brazil match ones in West Africa.

Glacial evidence. About 300 million years ago, glaciers scraped scratches into bedrock and left glacial deposits in South America, Africa, India, and Australia. Some of these places are near the equator today. When you put the continents back together, the scratches all point away from one ice sheet centered near the South Pole.

So why did geologists say no?

Wegener’s evidence showed that the continents had moved. It didn’t show how. Every scientific theory needs a mechanism: a process that explains how the thing happens. Wegener suggested the continents plowed through the ocean floor like ships through ice, driven by forces from Earth’s spin and the slow wobble of its axis. Physicists calculated that those forces were far too weak, and that continents couldn’t plow through solid ocean rock without crumpling. Most geologists, especially in North America, rejected the whole idea.

A few scientists kept working on it. In the late 1920s, the British geologist Arthur Holmes suggested that slow currents of heat in the mantle could carry continents along. It was a good guess, but there wasn’t enough evidence yet to test it. Wegener died on an expedition in Greenland in 1930, decades before his central idea was accepted.

Check yourself

  1. Why are Mesosaurus fossils such strong evidence that South America and Africa were once joined?
    Mesosaurus lived in fresh water, so it couldn’t have crossed a salty ocean. The simplest explanation is that it lived in one region that later split apart.
  2. Glacial scratches are found in parts of Africa and India that are near the equator today. What does that tell you?
    Those lands were once in a much colder place, near the South Pole. They have moved since then. When the continents are put back together, the scratches fit one ice sheet.
  3. Wegener had a lot of evidence. What was missing that kept most scientists from accepting his idea?
    A believable mechanism. He couldn’t explain what force could move continents, and the forces he suggested were much too weak.

Part 2

The seafloor tells the story

Until the 1940s, almost nobody knew what the ocean floor looked like. During and after World War II, ships used sonar to measure ocean depths, and the results were a surprise. In the 1950s, Marie Tharp and Bruce Heezen turned those measurements into the first detailed maps of the seafloor. Tharp noticed a deep valley running down the center of the mid-ocean ridge, an underwater mountain range that winds about 65,000 km around the globe.

Seafloor spreading

Around 1960, the geologist Harry Hess put the pieces together. He proposed seafloor spreading: magma rises at the mid-ocean ridge, cools into new ocean crust, and pushes older crust away on both sides. The seafloor moves away from the ridge like two conveyor belts heading in opposite directions. Hess also proposed that old seafloor sinks back into the mantle at deep ocean trenches. That’s how Earth makes new crust without getting any bigger. (You’ll study trenches in 4.2.)

If Hess was right, several things should be true. Scientists checked each one:

  • Age: Seafloor should be youngest at the ridge and get older with distance. Drilling ships in the late 1960s confirmed it. The oldest seafloor anywhere is only about 180 million years old, while some continental rocks are about 4 billion years old.
  • Sediment: Mud and ooze settle slowly onto the seafloor, so older crust should be buried under more of it. Sediment is thin at the ridge and thicker farther away.
  • Heat: More heat flows out of the seafloor at the ridge, where magma is close to the surface.

A magnetic barcode

The strongest evidence came from magnetism. Earth’s magnetic field is like a giant bar magnet, and every so often it flips. North becomes south and south becomes north. These magnetic reversals happen at irregular times, often hundreds of thousands of years apart. The last one was about 780,000 years ago.

When lava cools into basalt, tiny iron-rich minerals in it line up with Earth’s magnetic field, like compass needles, and get locked in place. The rock keeps a record of which way the field pointed when it formed. The study of this ancient magnetism is called paleomagnetism.

When ships towed magnetometers across the ocean, they found magnetic stripes: long bands of seafloor with normal polarity (like today) alternating with bands of reversed polarity. In 1963, Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews explained the pattern. New crust records the field at the ridge, then gets carried away on both sides. By the late 1960s, the evidence from the seafloor, earthquakes, and magnetism came together into one theory: plate tectonics. Earth’s outer shell is broken into large plates that move. The continents don’t plow through the ocean floor, as Wegener thought. They ride along as part of the plates.

Check yourself

  1. Where is the youngest rock on the ocean floor, and why?
    At the mid-ocean ridge. That’s where magma rises and cools into brand-new crust before it’s carried away.
  2. A band of seafloor 30 km east of a ridge has reversed polarity. Predict what you’d find 30 km west of the ridge, and explain.
    Reversed polarity, and rock of about the same age. Crust formed at the ridge splits and moves both ways at once, so the pattern is a mirror image.
  3. Why is the oldest seafloor (about 180 million years) so much younger than the oldest continental rock (about 4 billion years)?
    Ocean crust is constantly recycled. It forms at ridges and sinks back into the mantle at trenches, so very old seafloor no longer exists.

Part 3

Measuring the motion

Wegener could only argue that continents had moved. Today we can measure how fast. Every method uses the same idea you know from physics:

rate = distance ÷ time

Geologists usually measure distance in kilometers (km) and time in millions of years (My). That gives a rate in km per million years (km/My). Here’s a handy shortcut:

1 km/My = 1 mm/yr = 0.1 cm/yr

It works because 1 km is a million millimeters and 1 My is a million years. The millions cancel out.

Method 1: Magnetic stripes

The edges of magnetic stripes have known ages, measured by radiometric dating of the basalt. If you know how far a stripe edge is from the ridge and how old it is, you can calculate how fast that side of the seafloor moved.

Worked example

A stripe edge that formed 0.78 million years ago is now 20 km east of the ridge.

rate = 20 km ÷ 0.78 My ≈ 26 km/My = 26 mm/yr ≈ 2.6 cm/yr

That’s how fast the east side moves away from the ridge. It’s the half spreading rate. The west side moves away just as fast in the other direction, so the two plates separate at about 5.2 cm/yr. That’s the full spreading rate: always double the half rate.

Method 2: Hotspot tracks

A hotspot is a place where an unusually hot plume of material rises from deep in the mantle and melts through the plate above it. The hotspot stays roughly in place while the plate slides over it, so it builds a chain of volcanoes. The youngest volcano sits over the hotspot, and the others get older with distance.

The Hawaiian Islands are the classic example. The Big Island sits over the hotspot today. Kauai, to the northwest, has rocks roughly 5 million years old and is roughly 500 km away.

rate ≈ 500 km ÷ 5 My = 100 km/My = 100 mm/yr = about 10 cm/yr toward the northwest

Different sources give slightly different ages and distances, so you’ll see answers from about 9 to 11 cm/yr. That range is normal for real data.

Method 3: GPS

Stations using the Global Positioning System (GPS) can measure their position to within a few millimeters. Track a station for several years and you can watch the plate it sits on move. GPS stations in Hawaii show the Pacific plate moving northwest at about 7 cm/yr today. That’s close to the hotspot rate but not identical, because the hotspot rate is an average over 5 million years and GPS measures the last few decades.

Plates move at about the speed your fingernails or hair grow. Values are rounded.

Check yourself

  1. Crust that is 2.6 million years old is found 52 km from a ridge. What is the half spreading rate in cm/yr? What is the full spreading rate?
    52 km ÷ 2.6 My = 20 km/My = 20 mm/yr = 2 cm/yr (half rate). The full spreading rate is double: 4 cm/yr.
  2. A seamount formed over a hotspot 12.5 million years ago. It’s now 1,000 km from the hotspot. How fast has the plate moved, in cm/yr?
    1,000 km ÷ 12.5 My = 80 km/My = 80 mm/yr = 8 cm/yr.
  3. A GPS station moves 2.5 cm/yr. If that rate stayed the same, how far would it move in 1 million years?
    2.5 cm/yr = 25 mm/yr = 25 km/My, so it would move 25 km in 1 million years.

Part 4

What moves the plates?

This is the question that sank Wegener. The answer comes from Earth’s interior heat.

Heat on the move

Earth’s interior is hot for two reasons: heat left over from when the planet formed, and heat released by radioactive elements decaying in the rock. That heat is always moving outward toward the surface, in three ways:

  • Conduction: heat passes from particle to particle through a material that doesn’t move, like a metal spoon warming up in hot soup. Heat moves this way through the rigid outer layer of Earth.
  • Convection: warm material rises because it’s less dense, cools, becomes denser, and sinks again, carrying heat with it. Think of a pot of soup simmering. This is how heat moves through the mantle (and the liquid outer core).
  • Radiation: heat travels as electromagnetic waves, with no material needed. This is how the Sun’s energy reaches Earth and how Earth’s surface gives off heat to space.

A quick review of the layers

You already know Earth’s layers. For plate tectonics, two of them matter most. The lithosphere is the cold, rigid outer shell: the crust plus the very top of the mantle, usually around 100 km thick (thinner under ridges, thicker under continents). It’s broken into the plates. Below it is the asthenosphere, a hotter part of the upper mantle that is still solid rock but soft enough to flow very slowly, like extremely stiff putty. The plates slide over it.

Solid rock that flows?

It sounds wrong, but it’s true. Over millions of years, hot mantle rock creeps a few centimeters per year, the same pace as the plates. Glaciers do something similar: solid ice that slowly flows downhill.

The driving forces

Holmes’s old idea turned out to be on the right track. Mantle convection slowly carries heat upward from deep inside Earth. Hot material rises beneath ridges, and cold material sinks at trenches. But the plates aren’t just passive rafts on top of this flow. They’re part of it, and two forces act directly on them. Turn on each force in the diagram to see where it acts.

Select a force to read how it works.

Simplified cross section, not to scale.

  • Ridge push: New crust at a mid-ocean ridge is hot, so the ridge sits high. As the plate moves away it cools and sinks lower. Gravity makes the plate slide downhill, away from the high ridge.
  • Slab pull: As an ocean plate ages, it cools and becomes denser. At a trench, the old, cold, dense edge sinks into the mantle and drags the rest of the plate along behind it, like a tablecloth sliding off a table once enough of it hangs over the edge.

Geologists still debate exactly how much each force contributes, but most think ridge push and slab pull do most of the work. The best clue: plates attached to sinking slabs, like the Pacific, Australian, and Nazca plates, move the fastest, while plates without one, like the North American and African plates, move much more slowly.

Convection still matters, though. Without rising hot rock there would be no ridges to push from, and the North American plate keeps moving even though no part of it is sinking. (You’ll learn more about why ocean plates are denser in 4.2.)

Check yourself

  1. Which type of heat transfer moves heat through the mantle, and which moves it through the rigid lithosphere?
    Convection moves heat through the mantle, because the rock there can slowly flow. Conduction moves heat through the rigid lithosphere, which doesn’t flow.
  2. The asthenosphere is solid. How can plates slide over it?
    It’s hot and soft enough to flow very slowly over long periods, a few centimeters per year, even though it’s solid.
  3. The Pacific plate is one of the fastest plates, and much of its edge is sinking into trenches. The North American plate has no sinking edge and moves slowly. What does that comparison suggest?
    That slab pull is an important force. Plates with sinking slabs move faster than plates without them. But North America still moves, so other forces (ridge push and convection) must matter too.
